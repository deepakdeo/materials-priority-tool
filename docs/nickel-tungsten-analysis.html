This analysis maps the complete supply chain from ore to weapon system for two critical defense materials: ultra high purity nickel powder (used in superalloy turbine engine components, additive manufacturing, and advanced coatings) and tungsten powder (used in kinetic energy penetrators, cemented carbides for tooling, and counterweights). For each material, we identify the companies at every tier, from mining through refining, powder production, advanced material fabrication, component manufacturing, and final weapon system integration, and estimate material flows based on the 2026 USGS MCS and open-source industry data.

A critical January 2027 DFARS deadline [2] will prohibit DoD contractors from delivering items containing tungsten powder or tungsten heavy alloy mined, refined, or produced in China, Russia, Iran, or North Korea. This creates an urgent supply chain restructuring requirement.

4. Supply Chain Gap Analysis & Risk Assessment

Nickel: Moderate Risk, Allied Supply Adequate

  • Mining: Canada and Australia provide ~60% of U.S. import sources, allied nations with reliable supply [1]
  • Refining: No U.S. primary refinery since 1985 [1]; fully dependent on Canadian & Norwegian refineries; 3 refinery projects under development (MO, OK, TX) [1]
  • Powder: Strong domestic UHP powder production (HMI/RTX, Carpenter, 6K, ATI); aerospace-qualified
  • Advanced materials: World-class U.S. superalloy sector (PCC, Howmet, Special Metals, Haynes); minimal foreign dependency
  • Key gap: Refining step is the choke point: all Class I nickel for U.S. consumption is imported or refined abroad

Tungsten: HIGH Risk, Adversary Dominated

  • Mining: Zero U.S. production [1]; China produces ~80% globally [1]; 2025 Chinese export controls sharply reduced availability [7]
  • APT/Chemical: 7 U.S. converters exist but depend on imported feedstock (concentrate or APT) [1]; China still 26% of imports
  • Powder: Domestic capacity exists (GTP, Kennametal, Buffalo, Elmet) but constrained by feedstock supply
  • Products: U.S. fabrication capacity adequate for defense needs; GTP, Elmet, Kennametal can produce penetrators & WHA components
  • Key gap: Mining is the existential choke point: no domestic mine, ally/friend-shored mines (Sangdong, Mactung, Pilot Mountain) are years from full production

5. Methodology & Limitations

What DoD Comptroller Budget Data Provides

The P-1 (Procurement), R-1 (RDT&E), and detailed P-5/P-40 line item justifications give unit quantities and dollar amounts for weapon systems procured each fiscal year. This establishes the demand signal in end items, e.g., how many F-35 aircraft, 120mm tank rounds, or Tomahawk missiles DoD is buying. These are the essential "Layer 1" of any materials demand model.

What they do NOT provide: Bills of materials (BOMs), material composition by weight, or material intensity per unit. These are proprietary to prime contractors and typically classified or ITAR-controlled.

Translation Layer: End Items → Material Demand

The material quantities in this analysis are order-of-magnitude estimates derived from:

  • Published engine/airframe weight breakdowns and material composition studies (academic literature, OEM disclosures)
  • Publicly known ammunition specifications (round weights, penetrator dimensions, alloy compositions)
  • USGS consumption data and industry breakdowns by end-use sector
  • Industry publications (International Tungsten Industry Association, Nickel Institute)

Actual DoD material demand by type and amount is a classified or closely-held figure that DARPA's OPEN program [6] and DLA's RAMF-SM modeling attempt to quantify internally. As GAO has noted, "DOD doesn't have the information necessary to estimate all of its critical material needs." [16]

Recommended Approach for a Complete Analysis

  • Layer 1: DoD Comptroller P-1 line items → unit quantities by weapon system by FY
  • Layer 2: Material intensity coefficients → kg of each material per unit (from BOMs, literature, engineering estimates)
  • Layer 3: USGS MCS + trade data → domestic production, imports, exports, consumption by sector
  • Layer 4: NDS shortfall data + DLA RAMF-SM → emergency scenario demand vs. available supply
  • Layer 5: Gap = DoD demand − (domestic + allied supply − commercial competing demand − emergency attrition)
